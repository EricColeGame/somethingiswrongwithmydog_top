You know your dog better than anyone, so when something is wrong with my dog-style worries start creeping in — a weird gait, a refusal to eat, a look in her eyes you can't name — that instinct deserves to be taken seriously. The gap between "she seems off" and "we're at the vet" is where outcomes are decided, and most owners lose time not because they don't care, but because they aren't sure what counts as urgent. These survival tips are built around one idea: when something is wrong with my dog, calm, structured action beats panic every single time.

This guide walks you through triage, a quick at-home physical check, what to do (and never do) while you arrange care, and how to move a sick or injured dog without making things worse. It is educational information, not a substitute for veterinary advice — when in doubt, call your vet or the nearest emergency clinic and let a professional help you decide.

## Triage First: Signs That Mean "Go Now"

Before you search for anything else, sort the symptom into one of three buckets: emergency, urgent, or monitor. Owners routinely wait too long because early signs look mild from the outside. The table below covers the patterns that veterinary teams treat as time-critical.

| What you're seeing | What it may indicate | How fast to act |
|---|---|---|
| Labored breathing, blue or gray gums | Oxygen problem, heart or lung issue | Emergency — go now |
| Distended belly with unproductive retching | Bloat / GDV, a surgical emergency | Emergency — minutes matter |
| Collapse, seizure over 2–3 minutes, or clustered seizures | Neurologic or toxic cause | Emergency — go now |
| Straining to urinate with little or no output | Urinary blockage, especially in males | Emergency — go now |
| Pale, white, or bright red gums | Shock, blood loss, or fever | Emergency — go now |
| Bleeding that won't stop after 3–5 minutes of firm pressure | Deep wound or clotting problem | Emergency — go now |
| Known or suspected toxin exposure | Poisoning | Call poison control and your vet immediately |
| Vomiting or diarrhea once, bright and responsive | Often dietary upset | Monitor 12–24 hours with water available |
| Limping but bearing weight | Strain, sprain, or nail injury | Same-day call to your vet |
| Scratching, mild itch, slight odor | Skin or ear issue | Routine appointment |

The single most useful rule: if the behavior is new, severe, or getting worse by the hour, treat it as urgent until a vet says otherwise.

## The 10-Minute At-Home Check

You don't need equipment to gather useful data — you need ten quiet minutes and a notepad. Write down what you find and when it started; that timeline is genuinely valuable to your vet. Typical adult dog ranges are listed below, but keep in mind that size, age, and fitness shift these numbers, so your vet's guidance always wins.

| What to check | How to do it | Roughly normal for an adult dog |
|---|---|---|
| Gum color | Gently lift the lip | Bubblegum pink, moist |
| Capillary refill | Press a finger on the gum, release, count | Color returns in under 2 seconds |
| Heart rate | Hand flat on the chest behind the elbow | About 60–140 beats per minute |
| Breathing rate at rest | Count chest rises for 15 seconds, multiply by 4 | About 10–30 breaths per minute |
| Temperature | Rectal thermometer with a little lubricant | About 101–102.5°F |
| Hydration | Lift skin between the shoulder blades, release | Snaps back promptly |
| Mental state | Say her name, offer a treat | Alert and interested |

### Subtle Signs Owners Miss

In owner forums and community reports, the earliest warnings are rarely dramatic. They tend to be behavioral: a dog who suddenly hides, paces at night, stands with her head pressed against a wall, refuses to climb stairs she used to sprint up, drops food from one side of the mouth, or stops grooming. Any of these on its own can be nothing — three together is a phone call.

## Survival Tips That Buy You Time

While you're arranging care, your job is to keep the dog stable and avoid doing harm. Most of the damage in these hours comes from well-intentioned home remedies rather than the original problem.

| Do this | Skip this | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Call the clinic before you drive | Showing up unannounced at a clinic that isn't equipped for emergencies | Staff can prep and tell you where to go |
| Confine the dog to a small, quiet space | Letting her pace the house or follow you around | Rest lowers heart rate and slows bleeding |
| Write down the timeline and symptoms | Relying on memory at the front desk | Better history means faster decisions |
| Save the vomit, stool, or chewed packaging | Cleaning everything up before you leave | Helps identify toxins fast |
| Keep her warm with a light blanket | Applying heat pads directly to skin | Dogs in shock lose body heat quickly |
| Use a muzzle or slip lead if she's hurting | Assuming your sweet dog won't bite | Pain changes behavior in every animal |

### Never Do These at Home

- **Don't induce vomiting unless a vet or poison control tells you to.** It's dangerous with caustic substances, sharp objects, and some toxins.
- **Don't give human pain relievers.** Ibuprofen, naproxen, and acetaminophen are toxic to dogs at ordinary human doses.
- **Don't dose with human antihistamines or anti-diarrheals on your own.** Dosage depends on weight and the actual problem.
- **Don't force food or water** on a vomiting or lethargic dog.
- **Don't wait for morning** because the symptom seems mild at 11 p.m. Emergency clinics exist for exactly this.

If you suspect poisoning, contact the [ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control) or your vet's emergency line before you do anything else. Have the product label or plant name ready.

## Build a Dog Emergency Kit Before You Need It

The worst time to hunt for gauze is at midnight with a bleeding dog on your lap. A shoebox-sized kit in the car or hall closet covers the vast majority of home emergencies.

| Item | Why it earns a spot |
|---|---|
| Digital thermometer plus lubricant | Fever is one of the first measurable changes |
| Sterile gauze pads and rolled gauze | Pressure bandages for cuts and nail bleeds |
| Self-adhesive wrap (vet wrap) | Holds dressings without tape sticking to fur |
| Nonstick wound pads | Won't pull away from a raw wound |
| Blunt-tipped scissors and tweezers | Cut wrap, remove burrs and splinters |
| Saline flush | Rinses eyes, wounds, and mouths safely |
| Muzzle or slip lead | Every injured dog is a bite risk |
| Rigid board or thick blanket | Doubles as a stretcher for a down dog |
| Printed vet contacts and medication list | Saves minutes when you're flustered |
| Spare leash, towel, bottled water, bowl | Containment, warmth, and hydration |

Refresh the kit once a year, check expiration dates, and store a photo of your dog's vaccination records in your phone.

## Transporting a Sick or Injured Dog Safely

Moving a dog badly can turn a treatable injury into a permanent one — especially with suspected spinal or limb damage. Match your method to the situation.

| Situation | Safest approach | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Weak but walking | Sling a towel under the belly and support the hips | Sliding on hard floors |
| Small dog, unable to walk | Roll a blanket into a hammock and lift with two hands | Twisting the spine |
| Large dog, down | Rigid board, two people, one at the head and one at the hips | Dropping or dragging |
| Suspected spinal injury | Keep the body flat and the neck straight, no flexing | Twisting during the car ride |
| Bleeding limb | Firm pressure bandage, limb elevated if tolerated | Bandage cutting off circulation |
| Breathing difficulty | Keep her upright and sternal, no chest pressure | Windows down but head inside |

Call ahead so staff can meet you at the door, bring a second person if you can, keep the car cool and quiet, and drive smoothly rather than fast. If you're alone with a large dog, ask the clinic to send someone out — most will.

## FAQ

**Something is wrong with my dog and the vet is closed. What do I do first?**
Call the nearest 24-hour emergency clinic and describe the symptoms — most will triage you over the phone for free. If there's no emergency clinic nearby, call a telehealth vet service. For suspected poisoning, poison control hotlines operate around the clock and are often faster than driving.

**How do I tell the difference between an emergency and a bad day?**
Use the three-part test: is the symptom new, is it severe, and is it getting worse? Two out of three means call now. Breathing trouble, a distended abdomen, pale gums, collapse, and straining to urinate are always emergencies, no matter how cheerful your dog seems.

**Which survival tips matter most in the first hour?**
Keep the dog calm and confined, note the exact timeline, call ahead to the clinic, and avoid giving any medication. Those four steps prevent most of the harm that happens between noticing a problem and arriving for care.

**Can I give my dog something for pain while I wait?**
Not without veterinary direction. Common human pain relievers are genuinely dangerous for dogs, and the wrong choice can complicate treatment. Ask the clinic what, if anything, is safe for your dog's weight and symptoms — they answer this question constantly.

When something is wrong with my dog, the goal isn't to diagnose her yourself. It's to notice early, gather clean information, avoid harmful home fixes, and get her in front of someone who can help — quickly, calmly, and with everything they need to act.
